\begin{abstract}
Can a structured, pre-planned experiment both explain and optimize an XGBoost model, and how does that approach compare with adaptive search? We studied four hyperparameters on the California Housing regression task while measuring two outcomes: prediction error, expressed as root mean squared error (RMSE), and single-sample prediction latency. Lower values are preferred for both, so improving accuracy can conflict with improving speed.

The study used a 140-run face-centered central composite design planned in advance and repeated across five data-split blocks. This structure separated large hyperparameter effects from smaller changes caused by random train/validation partitions. It detected strong curvature, identified interactions, and produced quadratic response surfaces for candidate selection. Those surfaces were informative but imperfect: validation-RMSE lack of fit was significant, and the most accuracy-focused boundary candidate was more optimistic in the model than in independent confirmation. A balanced depth-4 candidate confirmed within its prediction interval, while a depth-7 accuracy candidate missed its interval by \numConfSoExceedance\ RMSE.

In a later 20-replicate comparison, single-objective TPE achieved the lowest observed mean holdout RMSE (\numRevSoTpeTestRMSE) but with higher latency (\numRevSoTpePredictLat\,$\mu$s). Repeated DOE and multi-objective TPE had no statistically significant difference in holdout RMSE (Welch $p=\numRevWelchPDoeMoMoTpe$) at similar latency; this non-significant result does not establish equivalence. The evidence supports a complementary interpretation: planned experiments are valuable for explanation, variance control, and stable structured selection, while adaptive search is effective at concentrating evaluations in promising regions. Results are limited to one dataset, four tuned factors, 100-tree XGBoost models, and a historically exposed holdout dataset.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} XGBoost, hyperparameter optimization, design of experiments, response surface methodology, prediction latency, multi-objective optimization.

\section{Introduction: the problem we want to solve}
XGBoost builds a prediction model by adding decision trees one after another \cite{chen2016xgboost}. Several settings---called \term{hyperparameters}---control how quickly the model learns, how complex each tree may become, how much training data each tree sees, and how strongly the model is regularized. Choosing these settings is consequential: a configuration can be accurate but slow, fast but inaccurate, or sensitive to the particular random data split used during evaluation.

Our research question is:
\begin{quote}
\textbf{Can a structured, pre-planned experimental design help us understand and optimize an XGBoost model, and how does that approach compare with adaptive optimization methods such as Optuna/TPE?}
\end{quote}

We measured two practical objectives. \term{Root mean squared error (RMSE)} summarizes the typical size of prediction errors in the target's units, while placing extra weight on large errors; \lowerbetter. \term{Inference latency} is the time required for one trained model to predict one observation, measured in microseconds; here too, \lowerbetter. A deeper or more complex model may reduce RMSE while taking longer to evaluate, so there is rarely one configuration that is best on both axes.

Finding one strong configuration was only part of the question. We also wanted to know which settings mattered, whether settings interacted, how results changed across random data partitions, whether a simple mathematical approximation was adequate, and how the accuracy--speed trade-off changed the preferred model. A planned \term{design of experiments (DOE)} provides that explanatory structure \cite{montgomery2017design}. Adaptive methods such as the \term{Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE)} instead use earlier trials to decide where to search next \cite{bergstra2011algorithms,akiba2019optuna}. Neither strategy is assumed to be universally superior; they spend information differently.

Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} shows the investigation from question to final comparison. The complete formulas, ANOVA tables, diagnostics, timing details, and provenance checks are retained in the companion technical supplement.

\section{Our core idea: learning from experiments}
An optimizer asks, ``Where should I try next to find a good model?'' A designed experiment also asks, ``What can this collection of trials teach us about the system?'' DOE chooses combinations so that changes in the response can be attributed to individual factors, interactions between factors, and curvature. \term{Response surface methodology (RSM)} then fits a low-order mathematical surface through those planned measurements \cite{box1951experimental,myers2016response}. The surface is a local guide: it can summarize broad tendencies and suggest candidates, but it must be checked against observations before being trusted.

We varied four XGBoost hyperparameters while holding the ensemble size at 100 trees:

\section{How we designed the experiment}
\subsection{Data partitions and holdout history}
The California Housing dataset contains 20,640 observations. A fixed 20\% external holdout contained 4,128 observations. The remaining 16,512 observations formed the development pool. Within each seed block, that pool was divided into 12,384 training and 4,128 validation observations. Search decisions used validation RMSE, while final generalization checks used the holdout test set.

During the repeated benchmark experiments, the selection procedure programmatically prevented access to holdout outcomes until the models were fixed for final evaluation. Earlier experiments had already reported results from the same holdout dataset, however. The holdout therefore cannot be described as untouched across the project's full history.

\subsection{Why blocking was necessary}
Changing a hyperparameter is not the only reason RMSE changes. A different random split can make a training problem slightly easier or harder. We therefore repeated planned configurations across five split seeds, called \term{blocks}. This allowed the statistical model to separate systematic hyperparameter effects from partition-related nuisance variation. Figure~\ref{fig:blocking} shows center-point observations: the configuration is nominally the same, yet the block means move.

The estimated block standard deviation was approximately \numSigmaBlock\ RMSE. Blocks accounted for an intraclass correlation of \numICCAnova\ (\numICCAnovaPct\ of unexplained residual variance), matching the REML estimate \numICCReml. This is not \numICCAnovaPct\ of total response variation; the main factor effects dominate the wide design region. It is nevertheless large enough to matter when competitive configurations differ by only a few thousandths of RMSE.
\begin{figure}[tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/explainer_blocking.pdf}
\caption{Why blocking matters. Canonical center-point measurements vary across data-split blocks even though the hyperparameters remain fixed. Repeated model seeds within a block show subsampling variation; movement between block means shows partition variation.}
\label{fig:blocking}
\end{figure}

\subsection{What the 140-run design means}
Each of five blocks contained 16 factorial corners, eight face-centered axial points, and four center runs, giving 140 model fits in total. All runs were generated in advance and globally randomized. In the shuffled execution order, \numDoeAxialBeforeHundred\ of the 40 axial runs happened before run 100. Thus the historical campaign was not an adaptive sequence in which an initial analysis triggered an augmentation.

The matched numerical budget needs qualification. DOE used 25 distinct coordinates across five blocks plus extra center replication; TPE and Random Search used 140 distinct configurations on one development split. DOE spent evaluations on variance estimation and structured effect identification. Adaptive search spent them on denser coordinate exploration. In addition, \numDoeLowEtaCount\ of the DOE's 140 runs (\numDoeLowEtaPct) lay at $\eta=0.01$, where 100 trees underfit: mean validation RMSE was \numDoeLowEtaMeanRMSE\ there versus \numDoeHighEtaMeanRMSE\ elsewhere.

\subsection{What we measured}
For each trial we recorded development validation RMSE and single-row CPU prediction latency. The initial measurements used warmup and 1,000 timed calls per prediction interface. The repeated searches used a cheaper 30-call online measurement, while final verification used two warmed 500-call sessions. Standard \texttt{predict} also includes data-wrapper overhead: the interface comparison measured \numRevInterfaceOverhead\,$\pm$\,\numRevInterfaceOverheadSd\,$\mu$s more than \texttt{inplace\_predict}. Search-time latency and benchmark verification latency are therefore distinct measurements and are never treated as interchangeable.

\section{How we learned from the experimental results}
\subsection{Screening and curvature}
The first analysis asked which effects were large enough to deserve attention. Learning rate dominated the first-order factorial model ($F=\numAnovaPOneFxone$), followed by depth ($F=\numAnovaPOneFxtwo$) and their interaction ($F=\numAnovaPOneFxoneXtwo$). An interaction means the effect of one setting depends on another: increasing depth does not have one constant consequence at every learning rate.

Center runs then tested whether a flat plane was adequate. The factorial mean exceeded the center mean by \numDiffCurv\ RMSE (\numCurvPctIncrease). Curvature was strong under every reported error denominator: $F=\numFCurv$ against center pure error, $F=\numCurvFBlockAdjPooled$ against pooled block-adjusted error, and $F=\numCurvFBlockPaired$ in the paired five-block analysis. The conclusion is non-planarity, not proof that a quadratic surface is globally correct. That distinction motivated the second-order fit and its adequacy tests.

\subsection{How settings shaped accuracy}
We wanted a mathematical picture of how learning rate and tree depth changed accuracy, rather than only a ranked list of configurations. Figure~\ref{fig:surface-rmse} shows a two-factor slice of the fitted quadratic surface. The horizontal axis is coded log learning rate, the vertical axis is coded maximum depth, and the contours and height show predicted validation RMSE; darker, lower regions are better. The surface falls sharply as learning rate moves away from the underfitting boundary, then forms a broad ridge. The cyan depth-7 candidate sits near the best in-domain region, while the stationary point indicated by the arrow lies outside the experimental domain. This geometry is why we optimized within the tested bounds and required confirmation.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/response_surface_rmse_2d_3d.png}
\caption{Empirical validation-RMSE response surface. Left: contours across coded log learning rate and depth at the single-objective slice. Right: the same fitted surface in three dimensions. The broad valley guides candidate selection, but the extrapolated stationary point lies outside the studied region.}
\label{fig:surface-rmse}
\end{figure}

The quadratic validation-RMSE model had $R^2=\numRsqRMSE$ and adjusted $R^2=\numAdjRsqRMSE$. These high values mainly reflect large changes across the full learning-rate range. Formal lack-of-fit remained significant: $F=\numFLofYone$ with $p=\numPLofYone$, and the lack-of-fit RMS scale was \numSqrtMSLofYone. When the severe $\eta=0.01$ boundary was removed, lack-of-fit sum of squares fell by \numLofRestrictedReductionPct, although the restricted model still did not justify treating the surface as exact.

The residual diagnostics, reproduced in the technical supplement, showed approximately normal residuals but also heteroscedasticity and localized influence. HC3 heteroscedasticity-consistent standard errors were used separately for coefficient inference; they do not remove model misspecification. Breusch--Pagan provided evidence of heteroscedasticity, while the serial diagnostics did not detect significant dependence in the tested residual order. Those diagnostics do not prove the absence of every temporal pattern or independently establish the absence of thermal drift.

\subsection{How model complexity affected speed}
The next question was whether deeper trees imposed a predictable latency cost. In Figure~\ref{fig:latency-depth}, the horizontal axis is maximum tree depth and the vertical axis is measured single-sample prediction time. The left panel compares empirical means and 95\% intervals with linear and quadratic fits; the right panel shows the full distribution at each tested depth. Latency rises modestly from depth 3 to 6 and much more sharply by depth 9, so a straight line understates the acceleration.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/response_surface_latency_2d_3d.png}
\caption{Measured prediction latency by maximum tree depth. The quadratic curve follows the accelerating cost better than the linear fit, while the boxplots show run-to-run variation around each tested depth.}
\label{fig:latency-depth}
\end{figure}

A quadratic depth profile achieved $R^2=\numLatQuadRsq$ compared with $R^2=\numLatLinRsq$ for a linear profile. Latency lack of fit was not significant ($F=\numFLofYtwo$, $p=\numPLofYtwo$). This result applies to the tested hardware and single-sample protocol, not to batch throughput or all deployment systems. The observed acceleration made depth central to the later accuracy--speed compromise.

\section{How we selected candidate models}
The fitted surface had a nearly flat direction: its smallest canonical eigenvalue was \numEigOne, with bootstrap interval $[\numBootLowEigOne,\numBootHighEigOne]$, and \numFracMinEigLeZero\ of bootstrap replicates placed it at or below zero. The unconstrained stationary point lay far outside the studied region. We therefore interpret the geometry as a stationary or rising ridge rather than a well-defined bowl with one stable interior minimum.

We used two selection goals. The \term{single-objective} candidate minimized predicted validation RMSE and moved toward a depth-7 boundary solution. The \term{multi-objective} candidate combined RMSE and latency through a Derringer--Suich desirability score \cite{derringer1980simultaneous}: each outcome was converted to a preference between zero and one, then combined. This selected an interior depth-4 compromise with learning rate \numOptEta, subsample \numOptSubsample, regularization \numOptLambda, and composite desirability \numOptD.

Figure~\ref{fig:desirability-pareto} shows how that decision emerged from the planned experiment. The horizontal axis is five-block mean validation RMSE and the vertical axis is five-block mean latency; moving left or down is better. Pale points are individual block observations, colored circles are configuration means, and the dashed staircase is the observed Pareto front. A point is \term{Pareto efficient} when no observed alternative is better on both outcomes. The depth-7 diamond pursues accuracy, whereas the depth-4 star gives up a small amount of predicted accuracy for much lower latency. That trade-off motivated carrying both candidates into confirmation.
\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/desirability_pareto_front.png}
\caption{Accuracy--speed evidence from the initial blocked experiment. Configuration means and their between-block uncertainty reveal the Pareto trade-off used to select the depth-4 compromise and depth-7 accuracy candidate.}
\label{fig:desirability-pareto}
\end{figure}

Sensitivity analysis changed desirability bounds and weights. The compromise moved as intended rather than producing one invariant optimum, which is why the desirability choice is treated as a declared decision rule rather than a discovered physical constant. Full sensitivity results remain in the technical supplement.

\section{How we checked whether the findings held up}
First, we evaluated the historical candidates across ten confirmation seeds:
\[
\mathcal{S}_{\text{conf}}=\{505, 606, 707, 808, 909, 1010, 1111, 1212, 1313, 1414\}.
\]
The intervals combined regression uncertainty with estimated block variance through a Satterthwaite approximation. This reflects uncertainty from a new partition, but it cannot guarantee protection against localized polynomial bias.

The depth-4 compromise produced validation RMSE \numConfEmpValRMSE\,$\pm$\,\numConfEmpValRMSEStd, inside its 95\% interval $[\numConfPiLowValRMSE,\numConfPiHighValRMSE]$. For the repeated benchmark experiments, every selected configuration was fixed before final evaluation. Each selection was then retrained across 20 seeds from 2001 through 2020 and assessed on the external holdout. Repeated DOE used 20 fresh search replicates, each containing five development blocks. Its single-objective rule selected the same predeclared grid point in every replicate, so between-search SD was \numRevDoeSoTestRMSESd; conditional retraining SD at that configuration was still \numRevDoeSoRetrainSd. These quantities describe different sources of variation and must not be conflated.

\section{Comparing DOE with alternative optimization methods}
We compared four adaptive or stochastic alternatives:
\begin{itemize}
\item \term{Random Search} samples configurations without learning from earlier results.
\item \term{Single-Objective TPE} adapts its sampling to reduce validation RMSE.
\item \term{Multi-Objective TPE} searches for configurations spanning the RMSE--latency trade-off.
\item \term{Constrained TPE} minimizes RMSE while treating 145\,$\mu$s as a latency constraint.
\end{itemize}

\section{What we learned}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.45em]
\item \textbf{Structured DOE explains variation well.} It identified dominant factors and interactions, quantified curvature, and separated an estimated split-related nuisance component from treatment effects.
\item \textbf{A response surface is a guide, not ground truth.} Strong global $R^2$ coexisted with significant local lack of fit and a failed confirmation interval at the boundary candidate.
\item \textbf{Adaptive search used the budget effectively for point optimization.} Single-objective TPE found the lowest observed RMSE, and multi-objective TPE produced strong development fronts by concentrating trials in promising regions.
\item \textbf{The deployment choice depends on the accuracy--speed objective.} The fastest acceptable and most accurate configurations were different; the holdout frontier contained selections from DOE and several TPE variants.
\item \textbf{Latency constraints require careful verification.} Constrained TPE's feasibility dropped from search to warmed benchmarking, showing that a noisy online timing estimate is insufficient near a hard threshold.
\item \textbf{The comparison remains limited.} DOE and TPE used equal fit counts but different coordinate replication, split structures, and timing protocols. The fixed holdout does not estimate dataset-sampling uncertainty, and prior historical exposure prevents describing it as untouched. Results come from one dataset, one hardware environment, four factors, and 100 trees. Stronger claims require fresh outer test splits or additional datasets, matched timing protocols, more model families, and designs that include the number of trees.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
A pre-planned blocked experiment can do more than produce a candidate configuration: it can show which hyperparameters matter, expose interactions and curvature, quantify random split effects, and reveal when its own approximation is inadequate. In this study, that explanatory value coexisted with clear limitations. The quadratic surface was not globally faithful, the accuracy-focused DOE candidate was optimistic in confirmation, and adaptive TPE found the lowest observed single-objective RMSE.

The multi-objective evidence does not identify one universal winner. Repeated DOE and multi-objective TPE produced similar observed holdout performance at comparable latency, with no statistically significant detected difference and no basis for an equivalence claim. The practical lesson is to combine the strengths of both approaches: use structured experiments when explanation, variance decomposition, and stable comparison matter; use adaptive search when dense exploitation of promising regions is the main goal; and independently confirm every selected model under the deployment measurement protocol.
